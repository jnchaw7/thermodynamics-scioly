Temperature Scales & the Zeroth Law

Thermal equilibrium, Kelvin/Celsius/Fahrenheit/Rankine, and temperature intervals.

Why it matters: Past tests often mix conceptual temperature questions with quick conversions, and the draft rules name the zeroth law and temperature scales explicitly.
Practice this rule areaJump to mini quiz

Zeroth law

  • If A is in thermal equilibrium with B, and B is in thermal equilibrium with C, then A is in thermal equilibrium with C.
  • This transitive property is what lets a thermometer serve as a reference system for measuring temperature.

Absolute vs relative scales

  • Kelvin is the SI unit of thermodynamic temperature. 0 K is absolute zero.
  • The Celsius degree and kelvin have the same interval size: a change of 1 °C equals a change of 1 K.
  • Fahrenheit intervals are 9/5 as large numerically as Celsius intervals. Rankine is an absolute scale with Fahrenheit-sized degrees.

Conversions

  • Use the offset only for absolute temperatures, not for temperature differences.
  • Never put a degree symbol on K. Write 300 K, not 300 °K.

Formula / memory box

K = °C + 273.15°F = (9/5)°C + 32°C = (5/9)(°F − 32)°R = °F + 459.67 = (9/5)K

Worked example

Prompt

Convert 25 °C to K and °F.

Solution

25+273.15=298.15 K; (9/5)(25)+32=77 °F.

MINI QUIZ

Practice before you leave

Try each one without opening the answer. Then reveal it to self-check.

Convert 100 °C to K.
Answer: 373.15 K.
Convert 68 °F to °C.
Answer: 20 °C.
A temperature rises by 20 °C. What is the rise in K?
Answer: 20 K.
A temperature rises by 18 °F. What is the rise in °C?
Answer: 10 °C.
Write absolute zero in °C.
Answer: −273.15 °C.
Why can a thermometer measure another object’s temperature?
Answer: At thermal equilibrium the thermometer and object have the same temperature; the zeroth law makes comparison transitive.
